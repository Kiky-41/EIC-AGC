**Equal Incremental Cost Method with Adjustable Gamma Control to Solve Generator Scheduling**

Rahmat, Basuki · Wijaya, I Gede Putu Oka Indra · Ikhsan, Rifki Rahman Nur · Yustika, Lindiasari Martha · Raharjo, Jangkung  
*Jurnal Infotel, 16(3), 2024*  
[![DOI](https://img.shields.io/badge/DOI-10.20895%2Finfotel.v16i3.1170-blue)](https://doi.org/10.20895/infotel.v16i3.1170)

Equal Incremental Cost method with Adjustable Gamma Control for generator scheduling (economic dispatch).

## Abstract

Generator scheduling remains an intriguing issue within the energy industry. It relates to the optimization of production costs, where system operators must select the optimal combination of available resources to minimize production costs. This paper proposes an enhancement to the Equal Incremental Cost (EIC) Method using Adjustable Gamma Control (AGC) in generator scheduling. Iterations begin with an initial lambda value, then gradually increase with the application of the  factor until power demand is met. A  variable of 10% is used as an adjustment step in the optimization method. The proposed method is capable of achieving convergence with 100% accuracy, where the power generated by all generators precisely matches the load demand (2,650 MW), at a cost of USD 32,289.03. EIC-AGC ranks second-best after VLIM, albeit with the consequence of consuming 195 seconds. This method is expected to have a significant impact on designing highly accurate economic dispatch techniques. Thus, generator scheduling will lead to a reduction in operational costs compared to current practices.



## Proposed method

EIC-AGC keeps the equal-incremental-cost (EIC) optimality condition of the classical lambda iteration and replaces the analytic lambda update with an explicit control step $\gamma$. The method starts from a common $\lambda$, advances it by $\gamma$ until total generation reaches the demand, then projects the schedule onto the ramp limits. A final repair loop removes the small residual mismatch in 0.01 MW increments. The paper tests $\gamma=10\text{ percent}$, $50\text{ percent}$ and $90\text{ percent}$; the reported run uses $10\text{ percent}$ and reaches the 2,650 MW demand exactly at USD 32,289.03.

### Problem formulation

The dispatch minimises total fuel cost,

$$\min F=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(a_i+b_iP_i+c_iP_i^2\right),$$

where each unit contributes a quadratic cost curve. The schedule must satisfy the power balance and the unit and ramp limits:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_i=P_D,\qquad P_i^{\min}\le P_i\le P_i^{\max},\qquad -R_i^{D}\le P_i^{t}-P_i^{t-1}\le R_i^{U}.$$

The first constraint fixes the total output; the second keeps every unit inside its nameplate range; the third limits how far a unit may move between two consecutive scheduling periods.

### Notation

| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| $N$ | number of generating units | – |
| $P_i$ | output of unit $i$ | MW |
| $P_i^{\min},P_i^{\max}$ | lower and upper generation limits | MW |
| $a_i,b_i,c_i$ | fuel-cost coefficients (constant, linear, quadratic) | USD/h, USD/MWh, USD/MW²h |
| $P_D$ | system demand | MW |
| $R_i^{U},R_i^{D}$ | ramp-up and ramp-down limits | MW per period |
| $F$ | total fuel cost | USD/h |
| $\lambda$ | common incremental cost (Lagrange multiplier) | USD/MWh |
| $\gamma$ | lambda control step | same unit as $\lambda$ |
| $t$ | scheduling period index | – |

### Update equations

Economic dispatch is optimal when the marginal costs of all unconstrained units are equal. Differentiating the cost curve gives the marginal cost of unit $i$:

$$\lambda_i(P_i)=\frac{dF_i}{dP_i}=b_i+2c_iP_i.$$

Setting $\lambda_i=\lambda$ and solving for $P_i$ yields the dispatch implied by a trial $\lambda$, with the result clipped to the unit limits:

$$P_i(\lambda)=\mathrm{clip}\left(\frac{\lambda-b_i}{2c_i},\quad P_i^{\min},\quad P_i^{\max}\right).$$

Instead of solving the balance equation analytically, EIC-AGC walks $\lambda$ upward in fixed steps:

$$\lambda^{(k+1)}=\lambda^{(k)}+\gamma.$$

A larger $\gamma$ reaches the demand in fewer iterations but can overshoot; a smaller $\gamma$ is more accurate but slower. After each step the schedule is made ramp-feasible against the previous period:

$$P_i\leftarrow\min\left(P_i^{t-1}+R_i^{U},\quad\max\left(P_i^{t-1}-R_i^{D},\quad P_i\right)\right).$$

If the clipped and ramp-limited schedule does not balance exactly, the residual is removed unit by unit. Writing $\Delta P_i=P_i-P_{i-1}$ for the step between neighbouring units, the unit with the largest positive step is reduced by 0.01 MW when generation is excessive, and the unit with the most negative step is raised when generation is short:

$$P_{i}\leftarrow P_{i}\pm0.01~\text{MW},\qquad i=\arg\max_i\Delta P_i~\text{or}~\arg\min_i\Delta P_i.$$

The loop stops when $\left|P_D-\sum_iP_i\right|\le10^{-5}$ MW.

### Algorithm

1. Set $\lambda=0$ and $P_i=P_i^{\min}$.
2. Increment $\lambda$ by $\gamma$; compute $P_i(\lambda)$ and clip to the generation limits.
3. Apply the ramp projection unit by unit.
4. Repair the residual balance in 0.01 MW steps until $\left|P_D-\sum_iP_i\right|\le10^{-5}$ MW.
5. Evaluate $F=\sum_i(a_i+b_iP_i+c_iP_i^2)$ and report the schedule.

### Implementation note

In the paper the ramp constraint couples consecutive hours. In the notebook the same clamp is applied sequentially between neighbouring units inside one hour, so the code is a unit-order heuristic rather than a strict inter-hour ramp check. The README formulation follows the paper.
### Reported result
The 15-unit, 2,650 MW case is dispatched exactly at USD 32,289.03 with a 10% step ($\gamma=10$); the paper also runs the same case at 50% and 90% control steps and compares the three schedules in cost and iteration count.

## Results

### 15-unit system, 2,650 MW, gamma sweep

| Gamma | Demand (MW) | Cost (USD/h) | Time (s) | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10% | 2,650 | 32,289.03 | 195 | finest step, best cost, slowest |
| 50% | 2,650 | 32,808.77 | 30.6 | coarser step, faster, cost rises |
| 90% | 2,650 | 33,126.20 | 25.5 | coarsest step, fastest, cost highest |

Cost and time trade off against the control step. A 10% step reaches 32,289.03 USD/h in 195 s, 50% costs 1.61% more and runs 6.4 times faster, 90% costs 2.59% more and runs 7.6 times faster. The extra fuel at large steps is the price of overshooting lambda and repairing the schedule afterwards.

### Same 2,650 MW case against published dispatch methods

| Method | Power (MW) | Cost (USD/h) | Time (s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| EIC-AGC | 2,650 | 32,289.03 | 195 |
| VLIM | 2,650 | 32,183.00 | 0.004 |
| ELIM | 2,650 | 32,542.00 | 0.12 |
| LIM | 2,650 | 32,549.00 | 2.6 |
| TVACPSO | 2,650 | 32,462.00 | 0.67 |
| MPSO | 2,650 | 32,465.00 | 1.87 |
| PSO | 2,650 | 32,476.00 | 0.76 |
| Dynamic programming | 2,650 | 32,506.00 | not reported |
| GSCNHGWO | 2,650 | 32,687.00 | 14.9 |

EIC-AGC is second only to VLIM (32,183.00 USD/h) and beats every published metaheuristic in the table, including TVACPSO at 32,462.00 USD/h. Run time is the weak side: 195 s is the slowest entry, about 40,000 times the 0.004 s of VLIM.
